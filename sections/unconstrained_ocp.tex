
\section{Unconstrained linear-quadratic OCP}

This section serves to explain the solution of the KKT system, which sits at the core of solving linear-quadratic optimal control problems.
We will first consider the unconstrained case, to gain a better understanding of the KKT system and it's importance to the problem.
The unconstrained finite-horizon linear-quadratic optimal control problem is formulated as follows.

\[
\begin{aligned}
    & \underset{x_k, u_k}{\text{minimize}} & & \sum_{k=0}^{N} \frac{1}{2} \vxuone{k}\T \Hcoststacked{k} \vxuone{k} \\
    & \text{subject to} & & x_0 = \hat{x}\\
    & & & x_{k+1} = A_k x_k + B_k u_k + b_k\\
\end{aligned}
\]

Note that \textit{unconstrained} in this case means that there are no \textit{inequality} constraints on states or inputs.
The only constraints are the system dynamics and the initial state, but these are primal feasibility constraints, which means they must be satisfied in order to attain a feasible solution.
They are thus not a constraint on the problem in the sense of restricting the solution space, rather an information to the problem about the underlying dynamics inherent to the system.
Let's look at how we can compute a solution to this problem.

\subsection{Solution via Equality Constraint Elimination}

The optimization problem is formulated such that the decision variables are the state and input pairs $(x_k, u_k)$ for all stages $k$ over the time horizon $N$.
Consecutive stages $k$ and $k+1$ are coupled, via the system dynamics in the form of the equality constraints

\[
x_{k+1} = A_k x_k + B_k u_k + b_k, \, \forall k\in\Iset{0}{N-1}
\]

This means that the state of a future stage $x_{k+1}$ is completely determined by the state of the current stage $x_k$ and the input of the current stage $u_k$.
This iteration is bounded by the initial condition $x_0 = \hat{x}$ at the begin of the time horizon.
This allows us to eliminate the states from the decision variables, leaving only the inputs at each stage as a decision variable, parametrized in the initial condition $x_0$.
To see this, let's explicitly calculate the state trajectory, starting at the initial stage.

\[
x_0 = \hat{x}
\]

The first stage is given by the first equality condition

\[
x_1 = A_0 \hat{x} + B_0 u_0 + b_0
\]

The second stage is given by the second equality condition

\[
x_2 = A_1 x_1 + B_1 u_1 + b_1
\]

We can now substitute the first stage into the second stage, eliminating $x_1$

\begin{align*}
    x_2 &= A_1 x_1 + B_1 u_1 + b_1 \\
    x_2 &= A_1 \left( A_0 \hat{x} + B_0 u_0 + b_0 \right) + B_1 u_1 + b_1 \\
    x_2 &= A_1 A_0 \hat{x} + A_1 B_0 u_0 + B_1 u_1 + A_1 b_0 + b_1
\end{align*}

We can do this for each stage and thus eliminate all states $x_k$.
The iteration can be written in the closed form for any given stage $k=j$ as

\[
x_j = \prod_{k=0}^{j-1} A_k \hat{x} + \sum_{k=0}^{j-1} \prod_{i=k+1}^{j-1} A_i B_k u_k + \sum_{k=0}^{j-1} \prod_{i=k+1}^{j-1} A_i b_k
\]

or in a less ugly and more readable form as vector matrix product

\[
\mathbf{x} = \Phi\,\hat{x} + \Gamma\,\mathbf{u} + \Theta\,\mathbf{b}
\]

where $\mathbf{x} = [x_1^\top, \ldots, x_N^\top]^\top$, $\mathbf{u} = [u_0^\top, \ldots, u_{N-1}^\top]^\top$, $\mathbf{b} = [b_0^\top, \ldots, b_{N-1}^\top]^\top$ and

\begin{align*}
    \Phi &= \begin{bmatrix} 
        A_0 \\ 
        A_1 A_0 \\ 
        \vdots \\ 
        \prod_{k=0}^{N-1} A_k 
    \end{bmatrix} \\
    \Gamma &= \begin{bmatrix} 
        B_0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 
        A_1 B_0 & B_1 & \cdots & 0 \\ 
        \vdots & \ddots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 
        \prod_{i=1}^{N-1} A_i\, B_0 & \cdots & A_{N-1} B_{N-2} & B_{N-1} 
    \end{bmatrix} \\
    \Theta &= \begin{bmatrix} 
        I & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 
        A_1 & I & \cdots & 0 \\ 
        \vdots & \ddots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 
        \prod_{i=1}^{N-1} A_i & \cdots & A_{N-1} & I 
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{align*}

This expression can be substituted back into the objective, leaving a condensed QP in only inputs $u_k$, parametrized in the initial condition $\hat{x}$.
Since the dimension of the problem has decreased from roughly $N*(n_x + n_u)$ to $N*n_u$, this process is known as \textit{condensing}.
The benefit of a smaller dimension, comes at the cost of a dense hessian.
The hessian after the condensing is given as

\[
\mathcal{H} = R + \Gamma\T Q \Gamma
\]

where $R:=diag(R_k)$ and $Q:=diag(Q_k)$.
Since this paper focuses on the implementation of sparsity exploiting methods, this section is thus concluded at this point

\subsection{Solution via Karush-Kuhn-Tucker}

In terms of numeric optimization, the OCP above is an equality-constrained QP, which is convex in the decision variables $x_k$ and $u_k$.
It is convex, because the cost function is quadratic in $x_k$ and $u_k$ with $Q_k \in \Sp{n_x}$ and $R_k - S_k\T Q_k^{-1} S_k \in \Spp{n_u \times n_u}$ per definition and the equality constraints are affine in $x_k$ and $u_k$.
Since the problem is convex, it has a unique solution.
This solution is sufficiently characterized by the famous Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions.
To derive the KKT conditions, we first form the Lagrangian by introducing the Lagrange multipliers $\pi_k \in \R{n_x}$ for the dynamic constraints.

\[
\mathcal{L}(x_k, u_k, \pi_k) = \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} l_k(x_k, u_k) + l_N(x_N) + \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \pi_{k+1}\T (A_k x_k + B_k u_k + b_k - x_{k+1})
\]

With the stage cost

\[
l_k(x_k, u_k) = \frac{1}{2} x_k\T Q_k x_k + \frac{1}{2} u_k\T R_k u_k + x_k\T S_k\T u_k + q_k\T x_k + r_k\T u_k
\]

and terminal cost

\[
l_N(x_N) = \frac{1}{2} x_N\T Q_N x_N + q_N\T x_N
\]

The KKT conditions are obtained by the stationary conditions, which say, that the Lagrangian becomes stationary at the optimal solution $(x_k^*, u_k^*, \pi_k^*)$ with respect to all primal and dual variables.

\[
\nabla \mathcal{L}(x_k^*, u_k^*, \pi_k^*) = 0
\]

The stationary conditions are given as follows.
Stationary w.r.t. states $x_k$ for $k\in\Iset{0}{N-1}$:

\[
\nabla_{x_k} \mathcal{L} = Q_k x_k + S_k u_k + q_k + A_k\T \pi_{k+1} - \pi_k = 0
\]

Stationary w.r.t. inputs $u_k$ for $k\in\Iset{0}{N-1}$:

\[ 
\nabla_{u_k} \mathcal{L} = R_k u_k + S_k\T x_k + r_k + B_k\T \pi_{k+1} = 0
\]

Stationary w.r.t. Lagrange multipliers $\pi_k$ for $k\in\Iset{1}{N}$:

\[
\nabla_{\pi_k} \mathcal{L} = A_{k-1} x_{k-1} + B_{k-1} u_{k-1} + c_{k-1} - x_k = 0
\]

If we stack the decision variables and Lagrange multipliers into a single vector, we can write the KKT conditions as a large linear system.
This system is called the KKT-system.
Here is an example showing the structure for $N=2$.

\[
\begin{bmatrix}
    Q_0   & S_0 &       &     &     & A_0\T &       \\
    S_0\T & R_0 &       &     &     & B_0\T &       \\
          &     & Q_1   & S_1 &     & -I    & A_1\T \\
          &     & S_1\T & R_1 &     &       & B_1\T \\
          &     &       &     & Q_2 &       & -I    \\
    A_0   & B_0 & -I    &     &     &       &       \\
          &     & A_1   & B_1 & -I  &       &       \\
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
        x_0 \\ u_0 \\ x_1 \\ u_1 \\ x_2 \\ \pi_0 \\ \pi_1
\end{bmatrix}
= 
-\begin{bmatrix}
    q_0 \\ r_0 \\ q_1 \\ r_1 \\ q_N \\ b_0 \\ b_1
\end{bmatrix}
\]

The system can be summarized in the following form, which is the standard system arising from optimal control problems.

\[
\begin{bmatrix} \mathcal{H}  & \mathcal{A} \T \\ \mathcal{A}  & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} y \\ \pi \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -h \\ -c \end{bmatrix}
\]

Looking closely at this system, we can see that the Hessians $\mathcal{H}$ is block-diagonal and $\mathcal{A}$ is block-bidiagonal.
That means the KKT system obeys a certain sparsity pattern.
This pattern can be exploited for fast and reliable solutions of the system, which is the core idea of tailored LQMPC solvers and is the main focus of this paper.
There are in general three approaches to solve this system.
The first and naive approach would be to feed this system to a dense symmetric-indefinite factorization solver.
This works, but finding the solution is of order $\mathcal{O}(N^3 \cdot (n_x + n_u)^3)$, i.e. cubic in the horizon and thus not relevant for practical applications.
The other two approaches are worth considering in a bit more detail.

\subsubsection{From KKT to Block-Cholesky}

The second approach is to notice the \textit{spacial} sparsity pattern of the hessian $\mathcal{H}$ and realize, the the solution to the system obayes a block-diagonal structure.
To show this, let us attempt to solve this system by eliminating y from the KKT system, via the schur complement.
We thus form

\[
y = \mathcal{H}^{-1}(-h - \mathcal{C}\T \pi)
\]

and substitute into $\mathcal{C}y = -c$, from which we get

\[
\mathcal{C} \mathcal{H}^{-1} \mathcal{C}\T \pi = \mathcal{C} \mathcal{H}^{-1} h - c
\]

We now define $Y := \mathcal{C} \mathcal{H}^{-1} \mathcal{C}\T$ and $\beta := c - \mathcal{C} \mathcal{H}^{-1} h$, to arrive at the system in the so called normal equations form.

\[
    Y \pi = \beta
\]

Let's closely observe the spacial structure of $Y$.
We see that the matrix $\mathcal{H}$ is block-diagonal with the stage hessian blocks

\[
H_k = \begin{bmatrix} Q_k & S_k \\ S_k\T & R_k \end{bmatrix}
\]

and the matrix $\mathcal{C}$ is block-bidiagonal.
Thus $Y$ has a block-tridiagonal structure inf the following way.

\[
Y = 
\begin{bmatrix}
    Y_{00}   & Y_{01}    &        &        \\
    Y_{01}\T & Y_{11}    & Y_{12} &        \\
             & Y_{12}\T  & Y_{22} & \ddots \\
             &           & \ddots & \ddots
\end{bmatrix}
\]

with $\Phi_k := H_k^{-1}$, the blocks of $Y$ are given as 

\[
Y_{k,k} = \begin{bmatrix} A_{k-1} & B_{k-1} \end{bmatrix} \Phi_{k-1} \begin{bmatrix} A_{k-1}\T \\ B_{k-1}\T \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} I & 0 \end{bmatrix} \Phi_k \begin{bmatrix} I \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
\]

\[
Y_{k,k+1} = -\begin{bmatrix} I & 0 \end{bmatrix} \Phi_k \begin{bmatrix} A_k\T \\ B_k\T \end{bmatrix}
\]

The next step is the cholesky factorization of $Y$
\[
Y = L_Y L_Y\T
\]

Due to the block-tridiagonal structure of $Y$, $L_Y$ is block-lower-bidiagonal:

\[
L_Y =
\begin{bmatrix}
    L_{00} &        &        & \\
    L_{10} & L_{11} &        & \\
           & L_{21} & L_{22} & \\
           &        & \ddots & \ddots
\end{bmatrix}
\]

This fact motivates a blockwise factorization with the following recursion

\[
L_{kk} L_{kk}\T = Y_{kk} - L_{k,k-1} L_{k,k-1}\T
\]

\[
L_{k+1,k} L_{kk}\T = Y_{k+1,k}
\]

Solve $L_Y z = \beta$ by forward substitution, solve $L_Y\T \pi = z$ by backward substitution and finally, recover $y = H^{-1}(-h - C\T \pi)$.

\subsubsection{From KKT to Dynamic Programming}

The third approach is to notice, that the problem also has temporal structure.
To be fair, the spacial structure we exploited above also stems from a temporal structure, but the interpretation takes on a different route.
Let's take a closer look at the KKT conditions to see the temporal relationship.
The stationary conditions with respect to $x_k$ can be rewritten as

\[
\pi_k = Q_k x_k + S_k u_k + q_k + A_k\T \pi_{k+1}
\]

This reveals that the dynamics of the costates $\pi_k$ propagate \textit{backward} in time.
It is bounded by the terminal condition $\pi_N = Q_N x_N + q_N$ at the end of the time horizon.
The stationary conditions with respect to $\pi_k$ reveal, that the dynamics of the states $x_k$ propagate \textit{forward} in time.

\[
x_{k+1} = A_k x_k + B_k u_k + b_k
\]

It is bounded by the initial condition $x_0 = \hat{x}$ at the begin of the time horizon.
Both dynamics are coupled via the stationary conditions for the inputs $u_k$

\[
R_k u_k + S_k\T x_k + r_k + B_k\T \pi_{k+1} = 0
\]

These equations form a coupled two-point boundary value problem (BVP).
They are not an initial value problem, which can be solved by forward integration.
Nor are they a final value problem, which can be solved by backward integration.
Suppose you try to solve it forward in time.
You start at stage $k=0$ with $x_0 = \hat{x}$.
To compute $u_0^*$, you need $\pi_1$.
But $\pi_1$ depends on $\pi_2$, which depends on $\pi_3$, all the way to $\pi_N$.
And $\pi_N$ depends on $x_N$, which you don't know because you haven't simulated forward yet.
Now suppose you try to solve it backwards.
You start at stage $k=N$ with $\pi_N = Q_N x_N + q_N$.
But again, you don't know $x_N$ without having simulated forward from $x_0$.
The information flows in opposite directions, and they're coupled through the optimality condition for $u_k$.
This is the essence of the two-point BVP.
Data is specified at the boundaries of the time horizon, and you can't simply iterate through in one direction.
The solution to this kind of problem comes from dynamic programming and Bellman's principle of optimality.
Using the dynamic programming approach is a fundamental shift of perspective.
Instead of searching for trajectories $x^*$, $u^*$ and $\pi^*$ that are characterized by the KKT conditions,
you are searching for an optimal policy, i.e. a function $u_k^*(x_k)$ parametrized in the state $xk$.
This will decouple the two-point BVP and allows for solving it in one backward (policy calculation) and one forward (trajectory rollout) iteration.

The key idea of dynamic programming is to reformulate the search for optimal \textit{trajectories} into a search for an optimal \textit{policy}, i.e.\ a function $u_k^*(x_k)$ that maps the current state to the optimal input.
This decouples the two-point BVP and allows the solution to be computed in one backward pass (policy computation) followed by one forward pass (trajectory rollout).

\paragraph{Bellman's Principle of Optimality.}
Consider the total cost-to-go from stage $k$ to the terminal stage $N$

\[
J_k(x_k, u_k, \ldots, u_{N-1}) = \sum_{j=k}^{N-1} l_j(x_j, u_j) + l_N(x_N)
\]

subject to the dynamics $x_{j+1} = A_j x_j + B_j u_j + b_j$.
The \textit{value function} $V_k(x_k)$ is defined as the minimum of this cost-to-go over all admissible input sequences

\[
V_k(x_k) = \min_{u_k, \ldots, u_{N-1}} J_k(x_k, u_k, \ldots, u_{N-1})
\]

Bellman's principle of optimality states:
\textit{An optimal policy has the property that whatever the initial state and initial decision are, the remaining decisions must constitute an optimal policy with regard to the state resulting from the first decision.}
Applied to our problem, this yields the recursive relationship known as the \textit{Bellman equation}

\[
V_k(x_k) = \min_{u_k} \left\lbrace l_k(x_k, u_k) + V_{k+1}(A_k x_k + B_k u_k + b_k) \right\rbrace
\]

with the terminal condition

\[
V_N(x_N) = l_N(x_N) = \frac{1}{2} x_N\T Q_N x_N + q_N\T x_N
\]

This equation is the foundation of the backward recursion.
At each stage $k$, the minimization is only over the \textit{current} input $u_k$.
All future costs are already captured in the value function $V_{k+1}$.
This is why dynamic programming breaks the curse of the coupled two-point BVP:
you never need to know $x_N$ explicitly.
You only need to know $V_{k+1}$ as a function of the state, and the dynamics do the rest.

\paragraph{Quadratic structure of the value function.}
Since the terminal cost is quadratic in $x_N$, and each stage adds quadratic terms and propagates through affine dynamics, we expect the value function to remain quadratic at every stage.
We thus make the ansatz

\[
V_k(x_k) = \frac{1}{2} x_k\T P_k x_k + p_k\T x_k + \eta_k
\]

with $P_k \in \Sp{n_x}$, $p_k \in \R{n_x}$ and $\eta_k \in \mathbb{R}$.
This ansatz will be verified by showing that if it holds at stage $k+1$, it also holds at stage $k$.
The terminal condition gives

\[
P_N = Q_N, \qquad p_N = q_N, \qquad \eta_N = 0
\]

\paragraph{Expanding the Bellman equation.}
Substituting the ansatz for $V_{k+1}$ and the stage cost $l_k$ into the Bellman equation, we obtain the \textit{action-value function} (or Q-function)

\[
\mathcal{Q}_k(x_k, u_k) := l_k(x_k, u_k) + V_{k+1}(A_k x_k + B_k u_k + b_k)
\]

such that $V_k(x_k) = \min_{u_k} \mathcal{Q}_k(x_k, u_k)$.
Let us expand this expression term by term.
The stage cost contributes

\[
l_k(x_k, u_k) = \frac{1}{2} x_k\T Q_k x_k + \frac{1}{2} u_k\T R_k u_k + u_k\T S_k\T x_k + q_k\T x_k + r_k\T u_k
\]

For the value function term, denote $\xi := A_k x_k + B_k u_k + b_k$.
Then

\begin{align*}
V_{k+1}(\xi) &= \frac{1}{2} \xi\T P_{k+1} \xi + p_{k+1}\T \xi + \eta_{k+1}
\end{align*}

Expanding $\xi\T P_{k+1} \xi$ gives

\begin{align*}
\xi\T P_{k+1} \xi &= (A_k x_k + B_k u_k + b_k)\T P_{k+1} (A_k x_k + B_k u_k + b_k) \\
&= x_k\T A_k\T P_{k+1} A_k x_k + u_k\T B_k\T P_{k+1} B_k u_k + b_k\T P_{k+1} b_k \\
&\quad + 2\, u_k\T B_k\T P_{k+1} A_k x_k + 2\, b_k\T P_{k+1} A_k x_k + 2\, b_k\T P_{k+1} B_k u_k
\end{align*}

and $p_{k+1}\T \xi = p_{k+1}\T A_k x_k + p_{k+1}\T B_k u_k + p_{k+1}\T b_k$.
Now collect all terms in $\mathcal{Q}_k(x_k, u_k)$ according to their dependence on $x_k$ and $u_k$.
The result is again a quadratic form in $(x_k, u_k)$

\[
\mathcal{Q}_k(x_k, u_k) = \frac{1}{2}
\begin{bmatrix} x_k \\ u_k \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\T
\begin{bmatrix}
    \tilde{Q}_k   & \tilde{S}_k  & \tilde{q}_k \\
    \tilde{S}_k\T & \tilde{R}_k  & \tilde{r}_k \\
    \tilde{q}_k\T & \tilde{r}_k\T & \tilde{\eta}_k
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} x_k \\ u_k \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
\]

with the \textit{expanded stage cost} matrices

\begin{align}
\tilde{Q}_k &= Q_k + A_k\T P_{k+1} A_k \label{eq:Qtilde} \\
\tilde{S}_k &= S_k + A_k\T P_{k+1} B_k \label{eq:Stilde} \\
\tilde{R}_k &= R_k + B_k\T P_{k+1} B_k \label{eq:Rtilde} \\
\tilde{q}_k &= q_k + A_k\T (P_{k+1} b_k + p_{k+1}) \label{eq:qtilde} \\
\tilde{r}_k &= r_k + B_k\T (P_{k+1} b_k + p_{k+1}) \label{eq:rtilde}
\end{align}

Note the structure:
these are the original stage cost matrices, augmented by the contribution of the future cost $V_{k+1}$ propagated backward through the dynamics.
The matrix $\tilde{Q}_k$ is the state cost after absorbing the future cost seen through $A_k$, $\tilde{R}_k$ is the input cost after absorbing the future cost seen through $B_k$, and $\tilde{S}_k$ captures the cross-coupling.

\paragraph{Minimization: the optimal policy.}
Since $\tilde{R}_k = R_k + B_k\T P_{k+1} B_k$ inherits positive definiteness from $R_k \succ 0$ and $P_{k+1} \succeq 0$, the Q-function is strictly convex in $u_k$ and the minimum is unique.
Setting $\nabla_{u_k} \mathcal{Q}_k = 0$ yields

\[
\tilde{R}_k u_k + \tilde{S}_k\T x_k + \tilde{r}_k = 0
\]

Solving for the optimal input gives

\begin{equation} \label{eq:optimal_policy}
u_k^* = - \tilde{R}_k^{-1} \left( \tilde{S}_k\T x_k + \tilde{r}_k \right) = K_k x_k + \kappa_k
\end{equation}

where we define the \textit{feedback gain} and \textit{feedforward term}

\begin{align}
K_k &:= -\tilde{R}_k^{-1} \tilde{S}_k\T \label{eq:gain} \\
\kappa_k &:= -\tilde{R}_k^{-1} \tilde{r}_k \label{eq:feedforward}
\end{align}

This is the optimal policy we were looking for: an affine state-feedback law, parametrized at each stage by the gain $K_k$ and the offset $\kappa_k$.
The gain $K_k$ is the \textit{LQR gain} when $q_k = 0$, $r_k = 0$ and $b_k = 0$.
The feedforward term $\kappa_k$ accounts for the affine terms in the dynamics and the linear terms in the cost.

\paragraph{The Riccati recursion.}
Substituting the optimal policy (\ref{eq:optimal_policy}) back into the Q-function and collecting terms in $x_k$, we verify the quadratic ansatz and obtain the recursion for $P_k$ and $p_k$.
The value function at stage $k$ is

\[
V_k(x_k) = \mathcal{Q}_k(x_k, u_k^*) = \frac{1}{2} x_k\T P_k x_k + p_k\T x_k + \eta_k
\]

To obtain $P_k$, collect all terms quadratic in $x_k$ from $\mathcal{Q}_k(x_k, K_k x_k + \kappa_k)$.
The quadratic contribution from $u_k^* = K_k x_k + \kappa_k$ is

\begin{equation*}
\frac{1}{2} x_k\T \left( \tilde{Q}_k + K_k\T \tilde{S}_k\T + \tilde{S}_k K_k + K_k\T \tilde{R}_k K_k \right) x_k
\end{equation*}

Substituting $K_k = -\tilde{R}_k^{-1} \tilde{S}_k\T$ and simplifying (using $K_k\T \tilde{R}_k K_k = \tilde{S}_k \tilde{R}_k^{-1} \tilde{S}_k\T$ and $K_k\T \tilde{S}_k\T + \tilde{S}_k K_k = -2 \tilde{S}_k \tilde{R}_k^{-1} \tilde{S}_k\T$) yields

\begin{equation} \label{eq:riccati_P}
P_k = \tilde{Q}_k - \tilde{S}_k \tilde{R}_k^{-1} \tilde{S}_k\T
\end{equation}

This is the \textit{discrete-time Riccati equation}.
Expanding the shorthand (\ref{eq:Qtilde})--(\ref{eq:Rtilde}), the fully explicit form reads

\[
P_k = Q_k + A_k\T P_{k+1} A_k - (S_k + A_k\T P_{k+1} B_k)(R_k + B_k\T P_{k+1} B_k)^{-1}(S_k + A_k\T P_{k+1} B_k)\T
\]

Similarly, collecting all terms linear in $x_k$ gives

\begin{equation} \label{eq:riccati_p}
p_k = \tilde{q}_k - \tilde{S}_k \tilde{R}_k^{-1} \tilde{r}_k
\end{equation}

and the constant term (which is not needed for computing the optimal trajectory, but completes the recursion) is

\[
\eta_k = \tilde{\eta}_k - \frac{1}{2}\tilde{r}_k\T \tilde{R}_k^{-1} \tilde{r}_k + \eta_{k+1}
\]

Equations (\ref{eq:riccati_P}) and (\ref{eq:riccati_p}) together with the definitions (\ref{eq:Qtilde})--(\ref{eq:rtilde}) and the terminal conditions $P_N = Q_N$, $p_N = q_N$ form the \textit{backward Riccati recursion}.
Note the Schur complement structure: $P_k$ is obtained from the expanded stage Hessian by eliminating the input block via the Schur complement with respect to $\tilde{R}_k$.
This is not a coincidence.
It is the matrix-level manifestation of the minimization over $u_k$.

\paragraph{Connection to the costates.}
The value function gradient with respect to the state is

\[
\nabla_{x_k} V_k(x_k) = P_k x_k + p_k
\]

We now show that this equals the costate $\pi_k$ from the KKT conditions.
Recall the costate dynamics

\[
\pi_k = Q_k x_k + S_k u_k + q_k + A_k\T \pi_{k+1}
\]

If we define $\pi_k := P_k x_k + p_k$ and substitute $u_k^* = K_k x_k + \kappa_k$ and $\pi_{k+1} = P_{k+1} x_{k+1} + p_{k+1}$ with $x_{k+1} = A_k x_k + B_k u_k^* + b_k$, the right-hand side becomes

\begin{align*}
&\phantom{=}\; Q_k x_k + S_k (K_k x_k + \kappa_k) + q_k + A_k\T \left[ P_{k+1}(A_k x_k + B_k (K_k x_k + \kappa_k) + b_k) + p_{k+1} \right] \\
&= \left[ Q_k + S_k K_k + A_k\T P_{k+1}(A_k + B_k K_k) \right] x_k \\
&\quad + \left[ S_k \kappa_k + q_k + A_k\T P_{k+1}(B_k \kappa_k + b_k) + A_k\T p_{k+1} \right]
\end{align*}

Using the definitions of $\tilde{Q}_k$, $\tilde{S}_k$ and $K_k = -\tilde{R}_k^{-1}\tilde{S}_k\T$, the coefficient of $x_k$ simplifies to

\begin{align*}
Q_k + S_k K_k + A_k\T P_{k+1}(A_k + B_k K_k)
&= \tilde{Q}_k + \tilde{S}_k K_k \\
&= \tilde{Q}_k - \tilde{S}_k \tilde{R}_k^{-1} \tilde{S}_k\T \\
&= P_k
\end{align*}

and the constant part simplifies to $p_k$ by an analogous calculation.
Thus $\pi_k = P_k x_k + p_k = \nabla_{x_k} V_k(x_k)$, which confirms the classical result:
\textit{the costate is the gradient of the value function}.
This is the link between the KKT perspective and the dynamic programming perspective.
The Riccati recursion computes the value function backward in time, which implicitly solves the backward costate dynamics.
The optimal policy $u_k^* = K_k x_k + \kappa_k$ then provides the missing coupling that decouples the two-point BVP into two sequential sweeps.

\subsection{The Riccati Recursion}

The dynamic programming argument of the previous section established that the
optimal cost-to-go is quadratic in the state,
\[
V_k(x_k) = \tfrac{1}{2} x_k\T P_k x_k + p_k\T x_k + \eta_k ,
\]
and that each backward step minimizes the sum of the stage cost and the
downstream cost-to-go over the current input. Writing this stage-wise
minimization out in closed form yields the \textit{Riccati recursion}, which
solves the unconstrained LQ-OCP in two sweeps over the horizon: a
\textit{backward pass} that propagates the value function and precomputes the
affine feedback law, followed by a \textit{forward pass} that rolls out the
optimal trajectory.

The backward pass is most transparent through the stage \textit{action-value}
(or $\mathcal{Q}$-) function
\[
\mathcal{Q}_k(x_k, u_k) := l_k(x_k, u_k) + V_{k+1}(A_k x_k + B_k u_k + b_k) .
\]
Substituting the value-function ansatz and collecting terms, $\mathcal{Q}_k$ is
quadratic with Hessian blocks and gradient and yields the tilde quantities below, which are simply the stage cost matrices augmented by
the propagated value function $(P_{k+1}, p_{k+1})$.

\paragraph{Backward pass ($k = N-1, N-2, \ldots, 0$).}
Initialize at the terminal stage with $P_N = Q_N$, $p_N = q_N$.
For each stage, first assemble the $\mathcal{Q}$-function blocks
\begin{align*}
\tilde{Q}_k &= Q_k + A_k\T P_{k+1} A_k \\
\tilde{S}_k &= S_k + B_k\T P_{k+1} A_k \\
\tilde{R}_k &= R_k + B_k\T P_{k+1} B_k \\
\tilde{q}_k &= q_k + A_k\T (P_{k+1} b_k + p_{k+1}) \\
\tilde{r}_k &= r_k + B_k\T (P_{k+1} b_k + p_{k+1}) ,
\end{align*}
then minimize $\mathcal{Q}_k$ over $u_k$ to obtain the affine feedback law
\begin{align*}
K_k &= -\tilde{R}_k^{-1} \tilde{S}_k \\
\kappa_k &= -\tilde{R}_k^{-1} \tilde{r}_k ,
\end{align*}
and finally update the value function by substituting the minimizer
$u_k^* = K_k x_k + \kappa_k$ back into $\mathcal{Q}_k$ (a Schur complement with
respect to the $\tilde{R}_k$ block)
\begin{align*}
P_k &= \tilde{Q}_k - \tilde{S}_k\T \tilde{R}_k^{-1} \tilde{S}_k = \tilde{Q}_k + \tilde{S}_k\T K_k \\
p_k &= \tilde{q}_k - \tilde{S}_k\T \tilde{R}_k^{-1} \tilde{r}_k = \tilde{q}_k + \tilde{S}_k\T \kappa_k .
\end{align*}
The right-hand forms reuse $K_k$ and $\kappa_k$, so only a single factorization
of $\tilde{R}_k$ is needed per stage. Observe that each tilde quantity inherits
the shape of its un-tilde counterpart, $\tilde{Q}_k \in \Sp{n_x}$,
$\tilde{R}_k \in \Spp{n_u}$ and $\tilde{S}_k \in \R{n_u \times n_x}$; in
particular $\tilde{S}_k$ carries the same orientation as the cross-weight $S_k$,
so the gain $K_k \in \R{n_u \times n_x}$ maps states to inputs directly.

\paragraph{Forward pass ($k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$).}
Starting from $x_0 = \hat{x}$, apply the precomputed feedback law and roll the
dynamics forward
\begin{align*}
u_k^* &= K_k x_k + \kappa_k \\
x_{k+1} &= A_k x_k + B_k u_k^* + b_k .
\end{align*}

If the costates are required (e.g.\ for constraint handling in an interior-point
method), they follow from the value-function gradient established earlier,
\[
\pi_k = \nabla_{x_k} V_k(x_k) = P_k x_k + p_k .
\]

\textit{Well-posedness and cost.}
The recursion is well defined as long as $\tilde{R}_k \in \Spp{n_u}$ at every
stage, which is guaranteed by the convexity assumptions on the OCP
($Q_k \in \Sp{n_x}$, positive definiteness of $R_k$ on the control subspace, and
$P_{k+1} \in \Sp{n_x}$ propagated from the terminal cost).
Per stage the work is dominated by propagating $P_{k+1}$ through the dynamics ---
the dense products $A_k\T P_{k+1} A_k$, $B_k\T P_{k+1} A_k$, $B_k\T P_{k+1} B_k$
--- together with the factorization of $\tilde{R}_k$ and the Schur-complement
product $\tilde{S}_k\T \tilde{R}_k^{-1} \tilde{S}_k$. Each is
$\mathcal{O}\!\left((n_x + n_u)^3\right)$, so the whole recursion costs
\[
\mathcal{O}\!\left(N (n_x + n_u)^3\right),
\]
i.e.\ \textit{linear} in the horizon length $N$ and cubic in the stage
dimension. This is the decisive structural gain over a dense factorization of
the full KKT system, which is $\mathcal{O}\!\left(N^3 (n_x + n_u)^3\right)$.

\paragraph{Practical Implementation.}
The recursion above propagates the cost-to-go matrix $P_k$ directly. The first and
most important refinement is to propagate a Cholesky factor $\Lambda_k$ instead,
with $P_k = \Lambda_k \Lambda_k\T$. The motivation is purely numerical: since
$\kappa(P_k) = \kappa(\Lambda_k)^2$, every operation carried out on the factor works
at the square root of the condition number of $P_k$ and hence loses only half as
many digits to rounding. The derivation rests on the observation that one backward
step is entirely a \emph{Gram-matrix} operation. Writing the stage Hessian as
$H_k = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} R_k & S_k \\ S_k\T & Q_k \end{smallmatrix}\right]$
and the input-major dynamics as $G_k = \begin{bmatrix} B_k & A_k \end{bmatrix}$, the
augmented Hessian is the congruence
\[
\tilde{\mathcal H}_k = H_k + G_k\T P_{k+1} G_k = H_k + W\T W ,
\qquad W := \Lambda_{k+1}\T G_k ,
\]
so substituting $P_{k+1} = \Lambda_{k+1}\Lambda_{k+1}\T$ replaces the propagation
$A\T P A$ by the formation of $W\T W$ --- the matrix $P_{k+1}$ is never assembled,
only its factor is read through the dynamics. It remains to extract $\Lambda_k$.
Ordering the Cholesky factorization of $\tilde{\mathcal H}_k$ in $[u;x]$ form gives a
block lower-triangular factor whose trailing block is precisely the factor of the
Schur complement,
\[
\tilde{\mathcal H}_k =
\begin{bmatrix} L_R & 0 \\ M & \Lambda_k \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} L_R\T & M\T \\ 0 & \Lambda_k\T \end{bmatrix} ,
\qquad
\Lambda_k \Lambda_k\T = \tilde Q_k - M M\T
                     = \tilde Q_k - \tilde S_k\T \tilde R_k^{-1} \tilde S_k = P_k .
\]
A single factorization thus realizes both the congruence and the Schur complement
that define a Riccati step, and the recursion maps factor to factor,
$\Lambda_{k+1} \mapsto \Lambda_k$, without $P$ ever appearing --- the discrete-time
LQ dual of square-root covariance (Kalman) filtering. The linear terms ride along as
a plain vector $p_k$, updated by a handful of matrix--vector products.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:sqrt-riccati} states the resulting recursion.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Square-root Riccati recursion --- Cholesky form}
\label{alg:sqrt-riccati}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require dynamics $A_k, B_k, b_k$; cost $Q_k, R_k, S_k, q_k, r_k$, $k \in \Iset{0}{N-1}$;
         terminal $Q_N, q_N$; initial state $\hat{x}$
\Ensure feedback $K_k, \kappa_k$; trajectory $x_k, u_k$; (optional) costates $\pi_k$
\Statex \textbf{Backward pass}
        \Comment{propagate the factor $\Lambda_k$ of the cost-to-go $P_k = \Lambda_k \Lambda_k\T$}
\State $\Lambda_N \gets \operatorname{chol}(Q_N)$,\quad $p_N \gets q_N$
\For{$k = N-1, \dots, 0$}
    \State $W \gets \Lambda_{k+1}\T \begin{bmatrix} B_k & A_k \end{bmatrix}$
           \Comment{\texttt{trmm},\; $W \in \R{n_x \times (n_u+n_x)}$}
    \State $w \gets \Lambda_{k+1}\!\left(\Lambda_{k+1}\T b_k\right) + p_{k+1}$
           \Comment{$w = P_{k+1} b_k + p_{k+1}$}
    \State $\tilde{\mathcal H} \gets
           \begin{bmatrix} R_k & S_k \\ S_k\T & Q_k \end{bmatrix} + W\T W$
           \Comment{\texttt{syrk}, lower triangle}
    \State $\begin{bmatrix} \tilde r \\ \tilde q \end{bmatrix} \gets
           \begin{bmatrix} r_k \\ q_k \end{bmatrix}
           + \begin{bmatrix} B_k\T \\ A_k\T \end{bmatrix} w$
           \Comment{\texttt{gemv}}
    \State factor $\tilde{\mathcal H} =
           \begin{bmatrix} L_R & 0 \\ M & \Lambda_k \end{bmatrix}
           \begin{bmatrix} L_R\T & M\T \\ 0 & \Lambda_k\T \end{bmatrix}$
           \Comment{\texttt{potrf}, $[u;x]$ order}
    \State $\ell \gets L_R^{-1}\tilde r$
           \Comment{\texttt{trsv}}
    \State $K_k \gets -L_R^{-\top} M\T$,\quad
           $\kappa_k \gets -L_R^{-\top}\ell$
           \Comment{\texttt{trsm}, \texttt{trsv}}
    \State $p_k \gets \tilde q - M\ell$
           \Comment{\texttt{gemv}}
\EndFor
\Statex \textbf{Forward pass}
\State $x_0 \gets \hat{x}$
\For{$k = 0, \dots, N-1$}
    \State $u_k \gets K_k x_k + \kappa_k$
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets A_k x_k + B_k u_k + b_k$
    \State \textit{(optional)}\; $\pi_k \gets \Lambda_k\!\left(\Lambda_k\T x_k\right) + p_k$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The form is fast because its inner loop leans on exactly the two kernels that vendor
and hand-tuned libraries optimize best: one symmetric rank-$n_x$ update
(\texttt{syrk}) to build $W\T W$, and one Cholesky (\texttt{potrf}) to factor the
result. Both are BLAS-3, both touch a single triangle, and for the small stage
dimension $n = n_u + n_x$ of embedded MPC both are cache-resident, so the recursion
is compute-bound on the FPU rather than memory-bound.

\paragraph{When Robustification Pays.}
The fast form makes one concession. Forming the normal matrix $W\T W$ explicitly
squares the conditioning of $W$: the matrix that \texttt{potrf} finally sees has
condition number $\kappa(W)^2$, so precisely at the point where the propagated factor
is squared back into a Gram matrix, half of the digits bought by carrying $\Lambda$
are conceded. Whether this matters is entirely a question of regime, and here the
speed--robustness frontier appears. Through the well-conditioned interior of the
interior-point method --- the large majority of iterations --- the concession is
invisible: $\tilde{\mathcal H}_k \succ 0$ comfortably and a few lost digits change
nothing. But an IPM deliberately drives its iterates toward the constraint boundary,
and as the slacks $s_i \to 0$ the barrier weights $S^{-1}\Lambda$ diverge like
$1/s_i$; the effective stage Hessian $\Phi_k = H_k + J_k\T S^{-1}\Lambda\, J_k$ that
the recursion actually factors grows ill-conditioned exactly in the late iterations
where the Newton direction must nonetheless remain accurate. There, the residual
squaring $\kappa(W)^2$ is the difference between a usable step and a numerically
singular one. Robustification is therefore not a global improvement --- it costs
arithmetic and a stronger premise on \emph{every} stage --- but a targeted one,
worthwhile only where conditioning is genuinely at risk. The two forms sit on a
Pareto frontier: neither dominates.

\paragraph{The Orthogonal Array Form.}
The array variant removes the last squaring. Factoring the stage Hessian once,
$H_k = L_{H_k} L_{H_k}\T$, and triangularizing the stacked pre-array with an
orthogonal $U$,
\[
U \begin{bmatrix} L_{H_k}\T \\ W \end{bmatrix}
  = \begin{bmatrix} \mathcal L_k\T \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} ,
\]
preserves the Gram matrix, $\mathcal L_k \mathcal L_k\T = L_{H_k} L_{H_k}\T + W\T W
= \tilde{\mathcal H}_k$ (since $U\T U = I$), and yields $\Lambda_k$ as the trailing
block of $\mathcal L_k$ --- but now no normal matrix is ever formed, so the step runs
at $\kappa(\Lambda)$ from end to end. Concretely $U$ is a product of $n_u + n_x$
Householder reflectors, each of height $1 + n_x$: the top block $L_{H_k}\T$ is already
triangular, so beneath each pivot sit only the $n_x$ rows of $W$. The price is a
strictly stronger premise --- the array factors the \emph{raw} $H_k$ and hence
requires $H_k \succ 0$, whereas the Cholesky form needs only the \emph{augmented}
$\tilde{\mathcal H}_k \succ 0$ (the $A\T P A$ term can lift a semidefinite $Q_k$ into
definiteness). Inside an interior-point method this distinction is moot: the barrier
renders $\Phi_k \succ 0$ at every interior iterate.

The linear terms $q_k, r_k, b_k$ can be threaded through the \emph{same} orthogonal
sweep by homogenizing the stage with the constant coordinate $1$. Appending $b_k$ to
the dynamics, $\bar G_k = \begin{bmatrix} B_k & A_k & b_k \end{bmatrix}$, and the
gradient $[r_k; q_k]$ to the Hessian turns the linear part of the value function into
one extra column of the pre-array. The subtlety is \emph{what} to propagate: the
naive homogeneous value matrix
\[
\bar P_k = \begin{bmatrix} P_k & p_k \\ p_k\T & 2\theta_k \end{bmatrix}
\]
is indefinite whenever the optimal cost-to-go
$V_k^\star = \theta_k - \tfrac{1}{2} p_k\T P_k^{-1} p_k$ is negative --- the generic
case once linear cost terms are present --- so it admits no real square root. This is
also why the fold is native to the array form and not to the Cholesky form: the
orthogonal sweep only triangularizes the \emph{genuine} columns and carries the
affine column along passively, never taking the square root of the homogeneous
corner, whereas a \texttt{potrf} on $\bar P_k$ would fail on exactly that pivot. The
remedy is to carry the linear term in \emph{factored coordinates}
$\nu_k := \Lambda_k^{-1} p_k$, the strictly-lower block of the homogeneous Cholesky
factor
\[
\begin{bmatrix} \Lambda_k & 0 \\ \nu_k\T & \gamma_k \end{bmatrix} ,
\qquad \gamma_k = \sqrt{2 V_k^\star} ,
\]
which is always real; the possibly-imaginary corner $\gamma_k$ is the cost constant
and is simply dropped, as it never enters the control law. With $\nu_k$ carried, the
appended column of the triangularized array emerges as $[\mu; \nu_k; \gamma_k]$ with
$\mu = L_R^{-1} \tilde r_k$, so the feedforward $\kappa_k = -L_R^{-\top} \mu$ and the
rolled $\nu_k$ are read off \emph{alongside} the gain --- the separate propagation of
$w$, $\tilde q_k$, $\tilde r_k$ and $p_k$ all collapse into the sweep. The costate,
if required, follows from $p_k = \Lambda_k \nu_k$ as
$\pi_k = \Lambda_k\!\left(\Lambda_k\T x_k + \nu_k\right)$.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Square-root Riccati recursion --- array form with affine fold}
\label{alg:sqrt-riccati-array}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require dynamics $A_k, B_k, b_k$; cost $Q_k, R_k, S_k, q_k, r_k$, $k \in \Iset{0}{N-1}$;
         terminal $Q_N, q_N$; initial state $\hat{x}$
\Ensure feedback $K_k, \kappa_k$; trajectory $x_k, u_k$; (optional) costates $\pi_k$
\Statex \textbf{Backward pass}
        \Comment{carry $\Lambda_k$ ($P_k = \Lambda_k \Lambda_k\T$) and $\nu_k := \Lambda_k^{-1} p_k$}
\State $\Lambda_N \gets \operatorname{chol}(Q_N)$,\quad
       $\nu_N \gets \Lambda_N^{-1} q_N$
       \Comment{\texttt{potrf}, \texttt{trsv}}
\For{$k = N-1, \dots, 0$}
    \State $L_H \gets \operatorname{chol}\!\begin{bmatrix} R_k & S_k \\ S_k\T & Q_k \end{bmatrix}$,\quad
           $h \gets L_H^{-1}\begin{bmatrix} r_k \\ q_k \end{bmatrix}$
           \Comment{\texttt{potrf} ($H_k \succ 0$), \texttt{trsv}}
    \State $W \gets \Lambda_{k+1}\T \begin{bmatrix} B_k & A_k \end{bmatrix}$,\quad
           $w_b \gets \Lambda_{k+1}\T b_k + \nu_{k+1}$
           \Comment{\texttt{trmm};\ \texttt{trmv}+\texttt{axpy}}
    \State assemble the pre-array \Comment{genuine cols $1{:}n$, affine col $n{+}1$; $n = n_u{+}n_x$}
    \Statex \qquad
           $\bar{\mathcal A} \gets
           \begin{bmatrix} L_H\T & h \\[2pt] W & w_b \end{bmatrix}
           \in \R{(n + n_x) \times (n + 1)}$
    \Statex \quad\emph{Householder triangularization of the genuine columns}
            ($\mathcal W$: the $n_x$ rows of the $W$-block):
    \For{$j = 1, \dots, n$}
        \State $v \gets \bar{\mathcal A}[\,\{j\} \cup \mathcal W,\; j\,]$,\quad
               $\alpha \gets -\operatorname{sign}(v_1)\,\lVert v \rVert$,\quad
               $v_1 \gets v_1 - \alpha$
        \State $\bar{\mathcal A}[\,\{j\} \cup \mathcal W,\; j{:}\,n{+}1\,] \gets
               \bigl(I - \tfrac{2}{v\T v}\, v v\T\bigr)\,
               \bar{\mathcal A}[\,\{j\} \cup \mathcal W,\; j{:}\,n{+}1\,]$
               \Comment{reflect trailing cols}
    \EndFor
    \State read the upper factor from rows $1{:}n$:\quad
           $\begin{bmatrix} L_R\T & M\T & \mu \\ 0 & \Lambda_k\T & \nu_k \end{bmatrix}
           \gets \bar{\mathcal A}[\,1{:}n,\; 1{:}\,n{+}1\,]$
    \State $K_k \gets -L_R^{-\top} M\T$,\quad
           $\kappa_k \gets -L_R^{-\top} \mu$
           \Comment{\texttt{trsm}, \texttt{trsv} on $L_R\T$}
\EndFor
\Statex \textbf{Forward pass}
\State $x_0 \gets \hat{x}$
\For{$k = 0, \dots, N-1$}
    \State $u_k \gets K_k x_k + \kappa_k$
    \State $x_{k+1} \gets A_k x_k + B_k u_k + b_k$
    \State \textit{(optional)}\; $\pi_k \gets \Lambda_k\!\left(\Lambda_k\T x_k + \nu_k\right)$
           \Comment{$p_k = \Lambda_k \nu_k$}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The cost of removing that last squaring is a constant factor, not an asymptotic
penalty. The orthogonal sweep replaces the \texttt{syrk}, and --- because the
pre-array requires $L_{H_k}\T$ on top --- the stage Hessian must be Cholesky-factored
on its own rather than folded in by a cheap addition. Both factorizations remain
$\tfrac13 n^3$ to leading order; exploiting the triangular top block, the reflector
sweep is $\mathcal O(n_x n^2)$, so the overhead sits near $1.3$--$1.4\times$ and is
strikingly stable in the ratio $n_x/n_u$, since the shared factorization and
\texttt{trmm} dominate. On a scalar FPU the more consequential cost is structural:
the inherently sequential reflector chain forgoes the BLAS-3 regularity and SIMD
friendliness of \texttt{syrk}\,+\,\texttt{potrf}.

Since both forms produce identical $(K_k, \kappa_k, \Lambda_k)$ and differ only in the
factorization kernel, the natural synthesis is to choose per stage and per iteration:
attempt the cheap Cholesky form, monitor a condition proxy on its result --- the
smallest pivot ratio $\min_i (L_R)_{ii}^2 / \lVert \tilde R_k \rVert$, or an
$\mathcal O(n^2)$ \texttt{pocon}-style estimate, cheaper than the factorization it
guards --- and fall back to the array kernel only on the stages and iterations where
the proxy trips. The fast path then carries the vast majority of well-conditioned
factorizations while the array form insures the ill-conditioned tail.
