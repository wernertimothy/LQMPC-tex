\section{Unconstrained linear-quadratic OCP}

This section serves to explain the solution of the KKT system, which sits at the core of solving linear-quadratic optimal control problems.
We will first consider the unconstrained case to gain a better understanding of the KKT system and its importance to the problem.
The unconstrained finite-horizon linear-quadratic optimal control problem is formulated as follows.

\[
\begin{aligned}
    & \underset{u_k, x_k}{\text{minimize}} & & \sum_{k=0}^{N} \frac{1}{2} \vxuone{k}\T \Hcoststacked{k} \vxuone{k} \\
    & \text{subject to} & & x_0 = \hat{x}\\
    & & & x_{k+1} = A_k x_k + B_k u_k + b_k, \quad k \in \Iset{0}{N-1}
\end{aligned}
\]

Note that \textit{unconstrained} in this case means that there are no \textit{inequality} constraints on states or inputs.
The only constraints are the system dynamics and the initial state.
They do restrict the feasible set, namely to an affine subspace in which the states are determined by the inputs and the initial state.
Unlike inequality constraints, however, they introduce no combinatorial structure (no active set), so the optimality conditions remain a linear system.
We now look at how to compute a solution to this problem.

\subsection{Solution via Equality Constraint Elimination}

The optimization problem is formulated such that the decision variables are the input and state pairs $(u_k, x_k)$ for all stages $k$ over the time horizon $N$.
Consecutive stages $k$ and $k+1$ are coupled via the system dynamics in the form of the equality constraints

\[
x_{k+1} = A_k x_k + B_k u_k + b_k, \, \forall k\in\Iset{0}{N-1}
\]

This means that the state of a future stage $x_{k+1}$ is completely determined by the state of the current stage $x_k$ and the input of the current stage $u_k$.
This iteration starts from the initial condition $x_0 = \hat{x}$ at the beginning of the time horizon.
This allows us to eliminate the states from the decision variables, leaving only the inputs as decision variables, parametrized by the initial condition $\hat{x}$.
To see this, let's explicitly calculate the state trajectory, starting at the initial stage.

\[
x_0 = \hat{x}
\]

The first stage is given by the first equality constraint

\[
x_1 = A_0 \hat{x} + B_0 u_0 + b_0
\]

The second stage is given by the second equality constraint

\[
x_2 = A_1 x_1 + B_1 u_1 + b_1
\]

We can now substitute the first stage into the second stage, eliminating $x_1$

\begin{align*}
    x_2 &= A_1 x_1 + B_1 u_1 + b_1 \\
    x_2 &= A_1 \left( A_0 \hat{x} + B_0 u_0 + b_0 \right) + B_1 u_1 + b_1 \\
    x_2 &= A_1 A_0 \hat{x} + A_1 B_0 u_0 + B_1 u_1 + A_1 b_0 + b_1
\end{align*}

We can do this for each stage and thus eliminate all states $x_k$.
The iteration can be written in closed form for any stage $j \in \Iset{1}{N}$ as

\[
x_j = \prod_{k=0}^{j-1} A_k \hat{x} + \sum_{k=0}^{j-1} \prod_{i=k+1}^{j-1} A_i B_k u_k + \sum_{k=0}^{j-1} \prod_{i=k+1}^{j-1} A_i b_k
\]

Here the products are ordered with decreasing index, $\prod_{i=k+1}^{j-1} A_i := A_{j-1} \cdots A_{k+1}$, and an empty product is the identity.
In matrix-vector form, the state trajectory reads

\[
\mathbf{x} = \Psi\,\hat{x} + \Gamma\,\mathbf{u} + \Theta\,\mathbf{b}
\]

where $\mathbf{x} = [x_1; \dots; x_N]$, $\mathbf{u} = [u_0; \dots; u_{N-1}]$, $\mathbf{b} = [b_0; \dots; b_{N-1}]$ and

\begin{align*}
    \Psi &= \begin{bmatrix} 
        A_0 \\ 
        A_1 A_0 \\ 
        \vdots \\ 
        \prod_{k=0}^{N-1} A_k 
    \end{bmatrix} \\
    \Gamma &= \begin{bmatrix} 
        B_0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 
        A_1 B_0 & B_1 & \cdots & 0 \\ 
        \vdots & \ddots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 
        \prod_{i=1}^{N-1} A_i\, B_0 & \cdots & A_{N-1} B_{N-2} & B_{N-1} 
    \end{bmatrix} \\
    \Theta &= \begin{bmatrix} 
        I & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 
        A_1 & I & \cdots & 0 \\ 
        \vdots & \ddots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 
        \prod_{i=1}^{N-1} A_i & \cdots & A_{N-1} & I 
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{align*}

This expression can be substituted back into the objective, leaving a condensed QP in the inputs $u_k$ only, parametrized by the initial condition $\hat{x}$.
Since the dimension of the problem has decreased from roughly $N(n_x + n_u)$ to $N n_u$, this process is known as \textit{condensing}~\cite{frison2013fast}.
The benefit of a smaller dimension comes at the cost of a dense Hessian.
To state the Hessian, we stack the stage weights as $\f{R} := \operatorname{blkdiag}(R_0, \dots, R_{N-1})$ and $\f{Q} := \operatorname{blkdiag}(Q_1, \dots, Q_N)$, matching the stacked states $x_1,\dots,x_N$.
The initial state $x_0 = \hat{x}$ is fixed and thus contributes to the parametric part of the problem only.
The same holds for the cross-term $u_0\T S_0 \hat{x}$ of the first stage.
The remaining cross-terms $u_k\T S_k x_k$, $k \in \Iset{1}{N-1}$, pair the input $u_k$ with the state $x_k$ and are collected in the block matrix $\bar{\f{S}} \in \R{N n_u \times N n_x}$, which has the blocks $S_1, \dots, S_{N-1}$ on its first block sub-diagonal and is zero elsewhere.
Note that the pairing is shifted by one index, because $x_k$ is the $k$-th block of $\mathbf{x}$, while $u_k$ is the $(k+1)$-th block of $\mathbf{u}$.
With this, the Hessian after the condensing is given as

\[
\Hcond = \f{R} + \Gamma\T \f{Q} \Gamma + \bar{\f{S}} \Gamma + \Gamma\T \bar{\f{S}}\T
\]

For the special case of no cross-terms, $S_k = 0$, this reduces to $\f{R} + \Gamma\T \f{Q} \Gamma$.
Since this paper focuses on sparsity-exploiting methods, we do not pursue condensing further.
For an in-depth discussion and implementation of this method, have a look at qpOASES~\cite{ferreau2014qpoases}.

\subsection{Solution via Karush--Kuhn--Tucker}

In terms of numerical optimization, the OCP above is an equality-constrained QP, which is strictly convex in the decision variables $u_k$ and $x_k$.
This holds because the cost function is quadratic in $u_k$ and $x_k$ with $H_k \succ 0$ for $k < N$ and $Q_N \succ 0$ by assumption and the equality constraints are affine in $u_k$ and $x_k$.
Strict convexity guarantees that the solution is unique.
This solution is fully characterized by the Karush--Kuhn--Tucker (KKT) conditions, which for a convex QP with affine equality constraints are necessary and sufficient.
To derive the KKT conditions, we first form the Lagrangian by introducing the Lagrange multipliers $\pi_0 \in \R{n_x}$ for the initial condition and $\pi_{k+1} \in \R{n_x}$ for the dynamic constraint of stage $k$.

\[
\mathcal{L} = \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} l_k(u_k, x_k) + l_N(x_N) + \pi_0\T (\hat{x} - x_0) + \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \pi_{k+1}\T (A_k x_k + B_k u_k + b_k - x_{k+1})
\]

With the stage cost

\[
l_k(u_k, x_k) = \frac{1}{2} u_k\T R_k u_k + \frac{1}{2} x_k\T Q_k x_k + u_k\T S_k x_k + r_k\T u_k + q_k\T x_k
\]

and terminal cost

\[
l_N(x_N) = \frac{1}{2} x_N\T Q_N x_N + q_N\T x_N
\]

The KKT conditions are the stationarity conditions, which state that the Lagrangian is stationary at the optimal solution $(u_k^*, x_k^*, \pi_k^*)$ with respect to all primal and dual variables.

\[
\nabla \mathcal{L}(u_k^*, x_k^*, \pi_k^*) = 0
\]

The stationarity conditions are given as follows.
Stationarity w.r.t. inputs $u_k$ for $k\in\Iset{0}{N-1}$:

\[ 
\nabla_{u_k} \mathcal{L} = R_k u_k + S_k x_k + r_k + B_k\T \pi_{k+1} = 0
\]

Stationarity w.r.t. states $x_k$ for $k\in\Iset{0}{N-1}$:

\[
\nabla_{x_k} \mathcal{L} = Q_k x_k + S_k\T u_k + q_k + A_k\T \pi_{k+1} - \pi_k = 0
\]

and for the terminal state:

\[
\nabla_{x_N} \mathcal{L} = Q_N x_N + q_N - \pi_N = 0
\]

Stationarity w.r.t. Lagrange multipliers $\pi_k$, which recovers the initial condition and the dynamics:

\[
\nabla_{\pi_0} \mathcal{L} = \hat{x} - x_0 = 0, \qquad
\nabla_{\pi_{k+1}} \mathcal{L} = A_k x_k + B_k u_k + b_k - x_{k+1} = 0, \quad k\in\Iset{0}{N-1}
\]

Introducing $\pi_0$ makes every state condition read uniformly with $-\pi_k$.
If we stack the decision variables and Lagrange multipliers into a single vector, we can write the KKT conditions as a large linear system.
This system is called the KKT system.
Here is an example showing the structure for $N=2$, with the variable ordering
\[
z = [u_0;\, x_0;\, u_1;\, x_1;\, x_2;\, \pi_0;\, \pi_1;\, \pi_2].
\]

\[
\begin{bmatrix}
    R_0   & S_0 &       &     &     &       & B_0\T &       \\
    S_0\T & Q_0 &       &     &     & -I    & A_0\T &       \\
          &     & R_1   & S_1 &     &       &       & B_1\T \\
          &     & S_1\T & Q_1 &     &       & -I    & A_1\T \\
          &     &       &     & Q_2 &       &       & -I    \\
          & -I  &       &     &     &       &       &       \\
    B_0   & A_0 &       & -I  &     &       &       &       \\
          &     & B_1   & A_1 & -I  &       &       &       \\
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
        u_0 \\ x_0 \\ u_1 \\ x_1 \\ x_2 \\ \pi_0 \\ \pi_1 \\ \pi_2
\end{bmatrix}
= 
-\begin{bmatrix}
    r_0 \\ q_0 \\ r_1 \\ q_1 \\ q_2 \\ \hat{x} \\ b_0 \\ b_1
\end{bmatrix}
\]

The system can be summarized in the following form, which is the standard system arising from optimal control problems.

\[
\begin{bmatrix} \mathcal{H}  & \mathcal{A} \T \\ \mathcal{A}  & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} y \\ \pi \end{bmatrix} = -\begin{bmatrix} h \\ c \end{bmatrix}
\]

with the primal vector $y = [u_0; x_0; \dots; u_{N-1}; x_{N-1}; x_N]$, the multipliers $\pi = [\pi_0; \dots; \pi_N]$, the gradient $h = [r_0; q_0; \dots; r_{N-1}; q_{N-1}; q_N]$ and the right-hand side $c = [\hat{x}; b_0; \dots; b_{N-1}]$.

Looking closely at this system, we can see that the Hessian $\mathcal{H}$ is block-diagonal with $\mathcal{H} = \operatorname{blkdiag}(H_0, \dots, H_{N-1}, Q_N)$ and $\mathcal{A}$ is block-bidiagonal.
That means the KKT system obeys a certain sparsity pattern.
This pattern can be exploited for fast and reliable solutions of the system, which is the core idea of tailored LQMPC solvers~\cite{wang2009fast,frison2020hpipm} and is the main focus of this paper.
There are in general three approaches to solve this system.
The first and naive approach would be to feed this system to a dense symmetric-indefinite factorization solver.
This works, but finding the solution is of order $\mathcal{O}\!\left(N^3 (n_x + n_u)^3\right)$, i.e.\ cubic in the horizon, which is impractical for all but short horizons.
The other two approaches are worth considering in a bit more detail.

\subsubsection{From KKT to Block-Cholesky}

The second approach is to notice the \textit{spatial} sparsity pattern of the Hessian $\mathcal{H}$ and realize that it carries over to the normal equations, which have a block-tridiagonal structure.
To show this, let us attempt to solve this system by eliminating $y$ from the KKT system, via the Schur complement.
We thus form

\[
y = \mathcal{H}^{-1}(-h - \mathcal{A}\T \pi)
\]

and substitute into $\mathcal{A}y = -c$, from which we get

\[
\mathcal{A} \mathcal{H}^{-1} \mathcal{A}\T \pi = c - \mathcal{A} \mathcal{H}^{-1} h
\]

We now define $Y := \mathcal{A} \mathcal{H}^{-1} \mathcal{A}\T$ and $\beta := c - \mathcal{A} \mathcal{H}^{-1} h$, to arrive at the system in the so-called normal equations form~\cite{wang2009fast}.

\[
    Y \pi = \beta
\]

We now examine the spatial structure of $Y$ more closely.
Here we need $\mathcal{H} \succ 0$, i.e.\ $H_k \succ 0$ for all stages including $Q_N \succ 0$, which holds by assumption.
Then every $H_k$ is invertible and, since $\mathcal{A}$ has full row rank, $Y \succ 0$ admits a Cholesky factorization.
We see that the matrix $\mathcal{H}$ is block-diagonal with the stage Hessian blocks

\[
H_k = \begin{bmatrix} R_k & S_k \\ S_k\T & Q_k \end{bmatrix}
\]

and the matrix $\mathcal{A}$ is block-bidiagonal.
With the stacked dynamics $G_k := \begin{bmatrix} B_k & A_k \end{bmatrix}$ and the state selector $E_k := \begin{bmatrix} 0 & I_{n_x} \end{bmatrix}$, such that $x_k = E_k y_k$ for the stage variable $y_k = [u_k; x_k]$, the block rows of $\mathcal{A}$ read $-E_0 y_0$ and $G_k y_k - E_{k+1} y_{k+1}$.
At the terminal stage there is no input, so $y_N = x_N$, $E_N = I_{n_x}$ and $H_N = Q_N$.
Thus $Y$ has a block-tridiagonal structure in the following way.

\[
Y = 
\begin{bmatrix}
    Y_{00}   & Y_{01}    &        &        \\
    Y_{01}\T & Y_{11}    & Y_{12} &        \\
             & Y_{12}\T  & Y_{22} & \ddots \\
             &           & \ddots & \ddots
\end{bmatrix}
\]

With $\Phi_k := H_k^{-1}$, the blocks of $Y$ are given as

\[
Y_{00} = E_0 \Phi_0 E_0\T, \qquad
Y_{k,k} = G_{k-1} \Phi_{k-1} G_{k-1}\T + E_k \Phi_k E_k\T \quad (k \geq 1)
\]

\[
Y_{k,k+1} = -E_k \Phi_k G_k\T
\]

The next step is the Cholesky factorization of $Y$
\[
Y = L_Y L_Y\T
\]

Due to the block-tridiagonal structure of $Y$, $L_Y$ is block-lower-bidiagonal:

\[
L_Y =
\begin{bmatrix}
    \LY{00} &         &         & \\
    \LY{10} & \LY{11} &         & \\
            & \LY{21} & \LY{22} & \\
           &        & \ddots & \ddots
\end{bmatrix}
\]

This fact motivates a blockwise factorization with the following recursion for $k = 0, \dots, N$, where the subtracted term is absent for $k = 0$ and the second equation applies only for $k < N$

\[
\LY{k,k} \LY{k,k}\T = Y_{k,k} - \LY{k,k-1} \LY{k,k-1}\T
\]

\[
\LY{k+1,k} \LY{k,k}\T = Y_{k+1,k}, \qquad Y_{k+1,k} = Y_{k,k+1}\T
\]

Finally, solve $L_Y \vY = \beta$ for the intermediate vector $\vY$ by forward substitution, solve $L_Y\T \pi = \vY$ by backward substitution, and recover $y = \mathcal{H}^{-1}(-h - \mathcal{A}\T \pi)$.
For an in-depth discussion and implementation of this method, have a look at FORCES~\cite{domahidi2012efficient}.

\subsubsection{From KKT to Dynamic Programming}
\label{sec:dp}

The third approach is to notice that the problem also has temporal structure.
Strictly speaking, the spatial structure exploited above also stems from a temporal structure, but the interpretation takes a different route.
A closer look at the KKT conditions reveals the temporal relationship.
The stationarity conditions with respect to $x_k$ can be rewritten as

\[
\pi_k = Q_k x_k + S_k\T u_k + q_k + A_k\T \pi_{k+1}
\]

This reveals that the dynamics of the costates $\pi_k$ propagate \textit{backward} in time.
They are fixed by the terminal condition $\pi_N = Q_N x_N + q_N$ at the end of the time horizon.
The stationarity conditions with respect to $\pi_k$ reveal that the dynamics of the states $x_k$ propagate \textit{forward} in time.

\[
x_{k+1} = A_k x_k + B_k u_k + b_k
\]

They are fixed by the initial condition $x_0 = \hat{x}$ at the beginning of the time horizon.
Both dynamics are coupled via the stationarity conditions for the inputs $u_k$

\[
R_k u_k + S_k x_k + r_k + B_k\T \pi_{k+1} = 0
\]

These equations form a coupled two-point boundary value problem (BVP)~\cite{bryson1975applied}.
They are not an initial value problem, which can be solved by forward integration.
Nor are they a final value problem, which can be solved by backward integration.
Consider solving it forward in time.
The iteration starts at stage $k=0$ with $x_0 = \hat{x}$.
Computing $u_0^*$ requires $\pi_1$.
But $\pi_1$ depends on $\pi_2$, which depends on $\pi_3$, all the way to $\pi_N$.
And $\pi_N$ depends on $x_N$, which is unknown before the forward simulation is complete.
Solving it backward fails for the same reason.
The iteration would start at stage $k=N$ with $\pi_N = Q_N x_N + q_N$, but $x_N$ is not known without simulating forward from $x_0$.
The information flows in opposite directions, and the two directions are coupled through the optimality condition for $u_k$.
This is the essence of the two-point BVP.
Data is specified at both boundaries of the time horizon, so the equations cannot simply be iterated in one direction.
The block-Cholesky approach above resolves this coupling by factoring the whole system at once.
Dynamic programming, based on Bellman's principle of optimality~\cite{bellman1957dynamic}, resolves it differently, and it is a fundamental shift of perspective.
Instead of searching for trajectories $x^*$, $u^*$ and $\pi^*$ that are characterized by the KKT conditions,
one searches for an optimal policy, i.e.\ a function $u_k^*(x_k)$ of the state $x_k$.
This decouples the two-point BVP and allows solving it in one backward pass (policy computation) and one forward pass (trajectory rollout).

\paragraph{Bellman's principle of optimality.}
Consider the total cost-to-go from stage $k$ to the terminal stage $N$

\[
J_k(x_k, u_k, \ldots, u_{N-1}) = \sum_{j=k}^{N-1} l_j(u_j, x_j) + l_N(x_N)
\]

subject to the dynamics $x_{j+1} = A_j x_j + B_j u_j + b_j$.
The \textit{value function} $V_k(x_k)$ is defined as the minimum of this cost-to-go over all admissible input sequences

\[
V_k(x_k) = \min_{u_k, \ldots, u_{N-1}} J_k(x_k, u_k, \ldots, u_{N-1})
\]

Bellman's principle of optimality states:
\textit{An optimal policy has the property that whatever the initial state and initial decision are, the remaining decisions must constitute an optimal policy with regard to the state resulting from the first decision.}~\cite[p.~83]{bellman1957dynamic}
Applied to our problem, this yields the recursive relationship known as the \textit{Bellman equation}~\cite{bertsekas1995dynamic}

\[
V_k(x_k) = \min_{u_k} \left\lbrace l_k(u_k, x_k) + V_{k+1}(A_k x_k + B_k u_k + b_k) \right\rbrace
\]

with the terminal condition

\[
V_N(x_N) = l_N(x_N) = \frac{1}{2} x_N\T Q_N x_N + q_N\T x_N
\]

The Bellman equation is the foundation of the backward recursion.
At each stage $k$, the minimization is only over the \textit{current} input $u_k$.
All future costs are already captured in the value function $V_{k+1}$.
This is how dynamic programming resolves the coupling of the two-point BVP:
$x_N$ never needs to be known explicitly.
Only $V_{k+1}$ is needed as a function of the state, and the dynamics do the rest.

\paragraph{Quadratic structure of the value function.}
Since the terminal cost is quadratic in $x_N$, and each stage adds quadratic terms and propagates through affine dynamics, we expect the value function to remain quadratic at every stage~\cite{bertsekas1995dynamic}.
We thus make the ansatz

\[
V_k(x_k) = \frac{1}{2} x_k\T P_k x_k + p_k\T x_k + \eta_k
\]

with $P_k \in \Sp{n_x}$, $p_k \in \R{n_x}$ and $\eta_k \in \mathbb{R}$.
This ansatz will be verified by showing that if it holds at stage $k+1$, it also holds at stage $k$.
The terminal condition gives

\[
P_N = Q_N, \qquad p_N = q_N, \qquad \eta_N = 0
\]

\paragraph{Expanding the Bellman equation.}
The expression minimized in the Bellman equation is the \textit{action-value function} (or $\mathcal{Q}$-function)~\cite{watkins1992q}

\[
\mathcal{Q}_k(u_k, x_k) := l_k(u_k, x_k) + V_{k+1}(A_k x_k + B_k u_k + b_k)
\]

such that $V_k(x_k) = \min_{u_k} \mathcal{Q}_k(u_k, x_k)$.
Substituting the ansatz for $V_{k+1}$, we expand it term by term.
The stage cost contributes

\[
l_k(u_k, x_k) = \frac{1}{2} u_k\T R_k u_k + \frac{1}{2} x_k\T Q_k x_k + u_k\T S_k x_k + r_k\T u_k + q_k\T x_k
\]

For the value function term, denote $\xi := A_k x_k + B_k u_k + b_k$.
Then

\begin{align*}
V_{k+1}(\xi) &= \frac{1}{2} \xi\T P_{k+1} \xi + p_{k+1}\T \xi + \eta_{k+1}
\end{align*}

Expanding $\xi\T P_{k+1} \xi$ gives

\begin{align*}
\xi\T P_{k+1} \xi &= (A_k x_k + B_k u_k + b_k)\T P_{k+1} (A_k x_k + B_k u_k + b_k) \\
&= x_k\T A_k\T P_{k+1} A_k x_k + u_k\T B_k\T P_{k+1} B_k u_k + b_k\T P_{k+1} b_k \\
&\quad + 2\, u_k\T B_k\T P_{k+1} A_k x_k + 2\, b_k\T P_{k+1} A_k x_k + 2\, b_k\T P_{k+1} B_k u_k
\end{align*}

and $p_{k+1}\T \xi = p_{k+1}\T A_k x_k + p_{k+1}\T B_k u_k + p_{k+1}\T b_k$.
Now collect all terms in $\mathcal{Q}_k(u_k, x_k)$ according to their dependence on $x_k$ and $u_k$.
The result is again a quadratic form in $(u_k, x_k)$

\[
\mathcal{Q}_k(u_k, x_k) = \frac{1}{2}
\begin{bmatrix} u_k \\ x_k \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\T
\begin{bmatrix}
    \tilde{R}_k   & \tilde{S}_k  & \tilde{r}_k \\
    \tilde{S}_k\T & \tilde{Q}_k  & \tilde{q}_k \\
    \tilde{r}_k\T & \tilde{q}_k\T & 2\tilde{\eta}_k
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} u_k \\ x_k \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
\]

with the \textit{augmented stage cost} terms

\begin{align}
\tilde{Q}_k &= Q_k + A_k\T P_{k+1} A_k \label{eq:Qtilde} \\
\tilde{S}_k &= S_k + B_k\T P_{k+1} A_k \label{eq:Stilde} \\
\tilde{R}_k &= R_k + B_k\T P_{k+1} B_k \label{eq:Rtilde} \\
\tilde{q}_k &= q_k + A_k\T (P_{k+1} b_k + p_{k+1}) \label{eq:qtilde} \\
\tilde{r}_k &= r_k + B_k\T (P_{k+1} b_k + p_{k+1}) \label{eq:rtilde} \\
\tilde{\eta}_k &= \tfrac{1}{2} b_k\T P_{k+1} b_k + p_{k+1}\T b_k + \eta_{k+1} \label{eq:etatilde}
\end{align}

Note the structure:
these are the original stage cost matrices, augmented by the contribution of the future cost $V_{k+1}$ propagated backward through the dynamics.
The matrix $\tilde{Q}_k$ is the state cost after absorbing the future cost seen through $A_k$, $\tilde{R}_k$ is the input cost after absorbing the future cost seen through $B_k$, and $\tilde{S}_k$ captures the cross-coupling.

\paragraph{Minimization: the optimal policy.}
Assume $P_{k+1} \succeq 0$, which holds for $k+1 = N$.
Then $\tilde{R}_k = R_k + B_k\T P_{k+1} B_k$ inherits positive definiteness from $R_k \succ 0$, so the $\mathcal{Q}$-function is strictly convex in $u_k$ and the minimum is unique.
Moreover, the Hessian of the $\mathcal{Q}$-function in $(u_k, x_k)$ is the \textit{augmented stage Hessian} $\tilde{H}_k := H_k + G_k\T P_{k+1} G_k \succeq H_k \succ 0$, with $G_k = \begin{bmatrix} B_k & A_k \end{bmatrix}$, and $P_k$ is its Schur complement with respect to $\tilde{R}_k$, see below.
Hence $P_k \succ 0$ by induction from $P_N = Q_N \succ 0$, so this holds at every stage.
Setting $\nabla_{u_k} \mathcal{Q}_k = 0$ yields

\[
\tilde{R}_k u_k + \tilde{S}_k x_k + \tilde{r}_k = 0
\]

Solving for the optimal input gives

\begin{equation} \label{eq:optimal_policy}
u_k^* = - \tilde{R}_k^{-1} \left( \tilde{S}_k x_k + \tilde{r}_k \right) = K_k x_k + \kappa_k
\end{equation}

where we define the \textit{feedback gain} and \textit{feedforward term}

\begin{align}
K_k &:= -\tilde{R}_k^{-1} \tilde{S}_k \label{eq:gain} \\
\kappa_k &:= -\tilde{R}_k^{-1} \tilde{r}_k \label{eq:feedforward}
\end{align}

This is the optimal policy we were looking for: an affine state-feedback law, parametrized at each stage by the gain $K_k$ and the offset $\kappa_k$.
The gain $K_k$ depends only on the dynamics, the quadratic weights and $P_{k+1}$, not on the linear terms or the offsets $b_k$: it is the time-varying \textit{LQR gain}~\cite{kalman1960contributions}.
The feedforward term $\kappa_k$ collects the affine terms in the dynamics and the linear terms in the cost, and it vanishes when $q_k$, $r_k$, $b_k$ and $q_N$ are all zero.

\paragraph{The Riccati recursion.}
Substituting the optimal policy \eqref{eq:optimal_policy} back into the $\mathcal{Q}$-function and collecting terms in $x_k$, we verify the quadratic ansatz and obtain the recursion for $P_k$ and $p_k$.
The value function at stage $k$ is

\[
V_k(x_k) = \mathcal{Q}_k(u_k^*, x_k) = \frac{1}{2} x_k\T P_k x_k + p_k\T x_k + \eta_k
\]

To obtain $P_k$, collect all terms quadratic in $x_k$ from $\mathcal{Q}_k(K_k x_k + \kappa_k, x_k)$.
The quadratic contribution from $u_k^* = K_k x_k + \kappa_k$ is

\begin{equation*}
\frac{1}{2} x_k\T \left( \tilde{Q}_k + K_k\T \tilde{S}_k + \tilde{S}_k\T K_k + K_k\T \tilde{R}_k K_k \right) x_k
\end{equation*}

Substituting $K_k = -\tilde{R}_k^{-1} \tilde{S}_k$ and simplifying (using $K_k\T \tilde{R}_k K_k = \tilde{S}_k\T \tilde{R}_k^{-1} \tilde{S}_k$ and $K_k\T \tilde{S}_k + \tilde{S}_k\T K_k = -2 \tilde{S}_k\T \tilde{R}_k^{-1} \tilde{S}_k$) yields

\begin{equation} \label{eq:riccati_P}
P_k = \tilde{Q}_k - \tilde{S}_k\T \tilde{R}_k^{-1} \tilde{S}_k
\end{equation}

This is the \textit{Riccati difference equation}.
For time-invariant data, its stationary solution satisfies the discrete algebraic Riccati equation (DARE) of the infinite-horizon LQR problem~\cite{bertsekas1995dynamic}.
Expanding the shorthand \eqref{eq:Qtilde}--\eqref{eq:Rtilde}, the fully explicit form reads

\[
P_k = Q_k + A_k\T P_{k+1} A_k - (S_k + B_k\T P_{k+1} A_k)\T(R_k + B_k\T P_{k+1} B_k)^{-1}(S_k + B_k\T P_{k+1} A_k)
\]

Similarly, collecting all terms linear in $x_k$ gives

\begin{equation} \label{eq:riccati_p}
p_k = \tilde{q}_k - \tilde{S}_k\T \tilde{R}_k^{-1} \tilde{r}_k
\end{equation}

and the constant term (which is not needed for computing the optimal trajectory, but completes the recursion) is

\[
\eta_k = \tilde{\eta}_k - \frac{1}{2}\tilde{r}_k\T \tilde{R}_k^{-1} \tilde{r}_k
\]

Equations \eqref{eq:riccati_P} and \eqref{eq:riccati_p} together with the definitions \eqref{eq:Qtilde}--\eqref{eq:rtilde} and the terminal conditions $P_N = Q_N$, $p_N = q_N$ form the \textit{backward Riccati recursion}.
The constant $\eta_k$, via \eqref{eq:etatilde} and $\eta_N = 0$, is only needed if the optimal cost itself is of interest.
Note the Schur complement structure: $P_k$ is obtained from the augmented stage Hessian $\tilde{H}_k$ by eliminating the input block via the Schur complement with respect to $\tilde{R}_k$.
This is not a coincidence.
It is the matrix-level manifestation of the minimization over $u_k$.

\paragraph{Connection to the costates.}
The value function gradient with respect to the state is

\[
\nabla_{x_k} V_k(x_k) = P_k x_k + p_k
\]

We now show that this equals the costate $\pi_k$ from the KKT conditions.
Recall the costate dynamics

\[
\pi_k = Q_k x_k + S_k\T u_k + q_k + A_k\T \pi_{k+1}
\]

If we define $\pi_k := P_k x_k + p_k$ and substitute $u_k^* = K_k x_k + \kappa_k$ and $\pi_{k+1} = P_{k+1} x_{k+1} + p_{k+1}$ with $x_{k+1} = A_k x_k + B_k u_k^* + b_k$, the right-hand side becomes

\begin{align*}
&\phantom{=}\; Q_k x_k + S_k\T (K_k x_k + \kappa_k) + q_k + A_k\T \left[ P_{k+1}(A_k x_k + B_k (K_k x_k + \kappa_k) + b_k) + p_{k+1} \right] \\
&= \left[ Q_k + S_k\T K_k + A_k\T P_{k+1}(A_k + B_k K_k) \right] x_k \\
&\quad + \left[ S_k\T \kappa_k + q_k + A_k\T P_{k+1}(B_k \kappa_k + b_k) + A_k\T p_{k+1} \right]
\end{align*}

Using the definitions of $\tilde{Q}_k$, $\tilde{S}_k$ and $K_k = -\tilde{R}_k^{-1}\tilde{S}_k$, the coefficient of $x_k$ simplifies to

\begin{align*}
Q_k + S_k\T K_k + A_k\T P_{k+1}(A_k + B_k K_k)
&= \tilde{Q}_k + \tilde{S}_k\T K_k \\
&= \tilde{Q}_k - \tilde{S}_k\T \tilde{R}_k^{-1} \tilde{S}_k \\
&= P_k
\end{align*}

and the constant part simplifies to $p_k = \tilde{q}_k + \tilde{S}_k\T \kappa_k$ by an analogous calculation.
The remaining KKT conditions hold as well: the terminal condition $\pi_N = Q_N x_N + q_N$ follows from $P_N = Q_N$ and $p_N = q_N$, and with $\pi_{k+1} = P_{k+1} x_{k+1} + p_{k+1}$ the input condition $R_k u_k + S_k x_k + r_k + B_k\T \pi_{k+1} = 0$ becomes $\tilde{R}_k u_k + \tilde{S}_k x_k + \tilde{r}_k = 0$, which the optimal policy satisfies by construction.
Thus $\pi_k = P_k x_k + p_k = \nabla_{x_k} V_k(x_k)$, which confirms the classical result~\cite{bryson1975applied}:
\textit{the costate is the gradient of the value function}.
This is the link between the KKT perspective and the dynamic programming perspective.
The Riccati recursion computes the value function backward in time, which implicitly solves the backward costate dynamics.
The optimal policy $u_k^* = K_k x_k + \kappa_k$ then provides the missing coupling that decouples the two-point BVP into two sequential passes.

\subsection{The Riccati Recursion}
\label{sec:riccati}

The dynamic programming argument of Section~\ref{sec:dp} established that the
optimal cost-to-go is quadratic in the state,
\[
V_k(x_k) = \tfrac{1}{2} x_k\T P_k x_k + p_k\T x_k + \eta_k ,
\]
and that each backward step minimizes the stage cost plus the downstream
cost-to-go over the current input. Writing this minimization out in closed form
yields the \textit{Riccati recursion}~\cite{frison2020hpipm,frison2015algorithms}, which solves the unconstrained LQ-OCP in
two passes over the horizon: a \textit{backward pass} that propagates the value
function and computes the affine feedback law, followed by a \textit{forward pass}
that rolls out the optimal trajectory.
The backward pass is built on the stage $\mathcal{Q}$-function
\[
\mathcal{Q}_k(u_k, x_k) := l_k(u_k, x_k) + V_{k+1}(A_k x_k + B_k u_k + b_k) .
\]
Its Hessian blocks and gradient are the tilde quantities below, i.e.\ the stage
cost matrices augmented by the propagated value function $(P_{k+1}, p_{k+1})$.

\paragraph{Backward pass ($k = N-1, N-2, \ldots, 0$).}
Initialize at the terminal stage with $P_N = Q_N$, $p_N = q_N$.
For each stage, first assemble the $\mathcal{Q}$-function blocks
\begin{align*}
\tilde{Q}_k &= Q_k + A_k\T P_{k+1} A_k \\
\tilde{S}_k &= S_k + B_k\T P_{k+1} A_k \\
\tilde{R}_k &= R_k + B_k\T P_{k+1} B_k \\
\tilde{q}_k &= q_k + A_k\T (P_{k+1} b_k + p_{k+1}) \\
\tilde{r}_k &= r_k + B_k\T (P_{k+1} b_k + p_{k+1}) ,
\end{align*}
then minimize $\mathcal{Q}_k$ over $u_k$ to obtain the affine feedback law
\begin{align*}
K_k &= -\tilde{R}_k^{-1} \tilde{S}_k \\
\kappa_k &= -\tilde{R}_k^{-1} \tilde{r}_k ,
\end{align*}
and finally update the value function by substituting the minimizer
$u_k^* = K_k x_k + \kappa_k$ back into $\mathcal{Q}_k$ (a Schur complement with
respect to the $\tilde{R}_k$ block)
\begin{align*}
P_k &= \tilde{Q}_k - \tilde{S}_k\T \tilde{R}_k^{-1} \tilde{S}_k = \tilde{Q}_k + \tilde{S}_k\T K_k \\
p_k &= \tilde{q}_k - \tilde{S}_k\T \tilde{R}_k^{-1} \tilde{r}_k = \tilde{q}_k + \tilde{S}_k\T \kappa_k .
\end{align*}
The right-hand forms reuse $K_k$ and $\kappa_k$, so $\tilde{R}_k$ is factored only
once per stage. Since $\tilde{S}_k \in \R{n_u \times n_x}$ has the same orientation
as the cross-weight $S_k$, the gain $K_k \in \R{n_u \times n_x}$ maps states to
inputs directly.

\paragraph{Forward pass ($k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$).}
Starting from $x_0 = \hat{x}$, apply the feedback law and roll the dynamics forward
\begin{align*}
u_k^* &= K_k x_k + \kappa_k \\
x_{k+1} &= A_k x_k + B_k u_k^* + b_k .
\end{align*}
If the costates are required, they follow from the value-function gradient,
\[
\pi_k = \nabla_{x_k} V_k(x_k) = P_k x_k + p_k .
\]

\paragraph{Classical implementation.}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:classical-riccati} implements the recursion by propagating the
cost-to-go matrix $P_k$ itself~\cite{frison2013efficient}. Each stage requires a single Cholesky factorization
$\tilde{R}_k = L_R L_R\T$, from which $M = \tilde{S}_k\T L_R^{-\top}$ yields both the
Schur complement $P_k = \tilde{Q}_k - M M\T$ and the feedback law.
The recursion is well defined as long as $\tilde{R}_k \in \Spp{n_u}$ at every
stage, which is guaranteed by the standing assumptions
$H_k \succ 0$ for $k < N$ and $Q_N \succ 0$: as shown in Section~\ref{sec:dp},
$\tilde{H}_k \succeq H_k \succ 0$, so $\tilde{R}_k \succ 0$ and $P_k \succ 0$ at
every stage.
These assumptions are sufficient, not necessary. Existence and uniqueness only
require $R_k\succ0$, $H_k\succeq0$ and $Q_N\succeq0$, under which the classical
recursion remains well defined with $P_k\succeq0$~\cite{frison2015algorithms}. These are the requirements
stated in Algorithm~\ref{alg:classical-riccati}.
Per stage, the work is dominated by propagating $P_{k+1}$ through the dynamics,
i.e.\ the dense products $A_k\T P_{k+1} A_k$, $B_k\T P_{k+1} A_k$, $B_k\T P_{k+1} B_k$,
together with the factorization of $\tilde{R}_k$ and the Schur-complement
product $\tilde{S}_k\T \tilde{R}_k^{-1} \tilde{S}_k$. Each is
$\mathcal{O}\!\left((n_x + n_u)^3\right)$, so the whole recursion costs
\[
\mathcal{O}\!\left(N (n_x + n_u)^3\right),
\]
i.e.\ \textit{linear} in the horizon length $N$ and cubic in the stage
dimension, compared to $\mathcal{O}\!\left(N^3 (n_x + n_u)^3\right)$ for a dense
factorization of the full KKT system~\cite{frison2013efficient}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Classical Riccati recursion}
\label{alg:classical-riccati}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require dynamics $A_k, B_k, b_k$; cost $Q_k, R_k, S_k, q_k, r_k$ with $R_k \succ 0$, $H_k \succeq 0$, $k \in \Iset{0}{N-1}$;
         terminal $Q_N \succeq 0$, $q_N$; initial state $\hat{x}$
         (sufficient for $\tilde{R} \succ 0$ in line~\ref{alg:classical-riccati:potrf})
\Ensure feedback $K_k, \kappa_k$; trajectory $x_k, u_k$; (optional) costates $\pi_k$
\Statex \textbf{Backward pass}
\Comment{propagate the cost-to-go $P_k$ itself, lower triangle}
\State $P_N \gets Q_N$,\quad $p_N \gets q_N$
\For{$k = N-1, \dots, 0$}
\State $\begin{bmatrix} \tilde{R} & \tilde{S} \\ \tilde{S}\T & \tilde{Q} \end{bmatrix} \gets
\begin{bmatrix} R_k & S_k \\ S_k\T & Q_k \end{bmatrix}
+ \begin{bmatrix} B_k\T \\ A_k\T \end{bmatrix} P_{k+1} \begin{bmatrix} B_k & A_k \end{bmatrix}$
\Comment{\texttt{symm}, \texttt{gemm}}
\State $w \gets P_{k+1} b_k + p_{k+1}$
\Comment{\texttt{symv}}
\State $\begin{bmatrix} \tilde r \\ \tilde q \end{bmatrix} \gets
\begin{bmatrix} r_k \\ q_k \end{bmatrix}
+ \begin{bmatrix} B_k\T \\ A_k\T \end{bmatrix} w$
\Comment{\texttt{gemv}}
\State factor $\tilde{R} = L_R L_R\T$
\label{alg:classical-riccati:potrf}
\Comment{\texttt{potrf}}
\State $M \gets \tilde{S}\T L_R^{-\top}$
\Comment{\texttt{trsm}}
\State $P_k \gets \tilde{Q} - M M\T$
\Comment{\texttt{syrk}, lower triangle}
\State $\zeta \gets L_R^{-1}\tilde r$
\Comment{\texttt{trsv}}
\State $\begin{bmatrix} K_k & \kappa_k \end{bmatrix} \gets
-L_R^{-\top} \begin{bmatrix} M\T & \zeta \end{bmatrix}$
\Comment{\texttt{trsm}}
\State $p_k \gets \tilde q - M\zeta$
\Comment{\texttt{gemv}}
\EndFor
\Statex \textbf{Forward pass}
\State $x_0 \gets \hat{x}$
\For{$k = 0, \dots, N-1$}
\State $u_k \gets K_k x_k + \kappa_k$
\State $x_{k+1} \gets A_k x_k + B_k u_k + b_k$
\State \textit{(optional)}\; $\pi_k \gets P_k x_k + p_k$
\EndFor
\State \textit{(optional)}\; $\pi_N \gets P_N x_N + p_N$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Square-root Riccati recursion.}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:classical-riccati} assembles $P_{k+1}$ explicitly and propagates
it through the dynamics at every stage. Propagating a Cholesky factor $\LP{k}$
instead~\cite{frison2013efficient}, with $P_k = \LP{k} \LP{k}\T$, is numerically preferable~\cite{kaminski1971discrete}: since
$\cond(P_k) = \cond(\LP{k})^2$, every operation carried out on the factor works
at the square root of the condition number of $P_k$ and hence loses only half as
many digits to rounding.
The derivation rests on the observation that one backward step is entirely a
\emph{Gram-matrix} operation. With the stage Hessian $H_k$ and the input-major
dynamics $G_k = \begin{bmatrix} B_k & A_k \end{bmatrix}$, the augmented stage
Hessian is $H_k$ plus a congruence of $P_{k+1}$,
\[
\tilde{H}_k = H_k + G_k\T P_{k+1} G_k = H_k + W\T W ,
\qquad W := \LP{k+1}\T G_k ,
\]
so substituting $P_{k+1} = \LP{k+1}\LP{k+1}\T$ replaces the congruence
$G_k\T P_{k+1} G_k$ by the Gram matrix $W\T W$. The matrix $P_{k+1}$ is never assembled,
only its factor is read through the dynamics. It remains to extract $\LP{k}$.
The Cholesky factorization of $\tilde{H}_k$ in $[u;x]$ order gives a
block lower-triangular factor whose trailing block is precisely the factor of the
Schur complement,
\[
\tilde{H}_k =
\begin{bmatrix} L_R & 0 \\ M & \LP{k} \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} L_R\T & M\T \\ 0 & \LP{k}\T \end{bmatrix} ,
\qquad
\LP{k} \LP{k}\T = \tilde Q_k - M M\T
                     = \tilde Q_k - \tilde S_k\T \tilde R_k^{-1} \tilde S_k = P_k .
\]
A single factorization thus realizes both the congruence and the Schur complement
that define a Riccati step, and the recursion maps factor to factor,
$\LP{k+1} \mapsto \LP{k}$, without $P$ ever appearing. This is the discrete-time
LQ dual of square-root covariance (Kalman) filtering~\cite{kalman1960new,kaminski1971discrete}. The linear terms ride along as
a plain vector $p_k$, updated by a few matrix--vector products.

The factorization above is also where the input-major ordering $[u;x]$ of the
stage variables pays off~\cite{frison2020hpipm}. A Cholesky factorization eliminates the variables in
index order, so the trailing block of a lower-triangular factor is always the
factor of the Schur complement of the leading block. One Riccati step is precisely
the Schur complement of $\tilde{H}_k$ with respect to $\tilde{R}_k$, i.e.\ the
elimination of $u_k$. With $u_k$ ordered first, a standard lower Cholesky
factorization (\texttt{potrf}) therefore returns $\LP{k}$ as its trailing block,
ready to be used in the next stage. In the state-major ordering $[x;u]$, the same
factorization would eliminate $x_k$ first and return the factor of
$\tilde{R}_k - \tilde{S}_k \tilde{Q}_k^{-1} \tilde{S}_k\T$, which is not the
cost-to-go. The upper variant $\tilde{H}_k = U\T U$ offers no way out, since
$U = L\T$ describes the same elimination order. One would need a reverse
factorization $\tilde{H}_k = U U\T$, which standard LAPACK does not provide, or an
explicit permutation at every stage. The classical form is indifferent to the
ordering, because it addresses the blocks $\tilde{R}_k$, $\tilde{S}_k$ and
$\tilde{Q}_k$ explicitly.

Note that one squaring remains: $\tilde{H}_k = H_k + W\T W$ is still formed
explicitly, so the factorization in line~\ref{alg:sqrt-riccati:potrf} of
Algorithm~\ref{alg:sqrt-riccati} operates on a
Gram matrix and concedes part of the digits gained by carrying $\LP{k}$. This is
immaterial as long as $\tilde{H}_k$ is reasonably well conditioned.

Since the full $\tilde{H}_k$ is factored, the square-root form needs
$\tilde{H}_k \succ 0$ rather than only $\tilde{R}_k \succ 0$, as well as
$Q_N \succ 0$ for the initial factorization. Under the standing assumptions this
holds by the argument given for the classical form. Under the weaker assumptions
$R_k\succ0$ and $H_k\succeq0$, with $Q_N\succ0$ still required, and given $P_{k+1}\succ0$,
it holds if and only if
$\ker(Q_k-S_k\T R_k^{-1}S_k)\cap\ker(A_k-B_kR_k^{-1}S_k)=\{0\}$.
If this holds at every stage, $P_k \succ 0$ follows by induction.
For $S_k=0$ the condition reads $\ker Q_k\cap\ker A_k=\{0\}$, which holds for any rank-deficient
$Q_k\succeq0$ as long as $A_k$ is invertible, as it is for the zero-order-hold
discretization of a continuous-time model. Delay states modeled as a shift register
are the typical exception.
If the condition fails, a regularization $Q_k+\varepsilon I$ restores it.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:sqrt-riccati} states the resulting recursion.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Square-root Riccati recursion --- Cholesky form}
\label{alg:sqrt-riccati}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require dynamics $A_k, B_k, b_k$; cost $Q_k, R_k, S_k, q_k, r_k$ with $H_k \succ 0$, $k \in \Iset{0}{N-1}$;
         terminal $Q_N \succ 0$, $q_N$; initial state $\hat{x}$
         (sufficient for $\tilde{H} \succ 0$ in line~\ref{alg:sqrt-riccati:potrf})
\Ensure feedback $K_k, \kappa_k$; trajectory $x_k, u_k$; (optional) costates $\pi_k$
\Statex \textbf{Backward pass}
\Comment{propagate the factor $\LP{k}$ of the cost-to-go $P_k = \LP{k} \LP{k}\T$}
\State factor $Q_N = \LP{N} \LP{N}\T$,\quad $p_N \gets q_N$
\Comment{\texttt{potrf}}
\For{$k = N-1, \dots, 0$}
\State $W \gets \LP{k+1}\T \begin{bmatrix} B_k & A_k \end{bmatrix}$
\Comment{\texttt{trmm},\; $W \in \R{n_x \times (n_u+n_x)}$}
\State $w \gets \LP{k+1}\!\left(\LP{k+1}\T b_k\right) + p_{k+1}$
\Comment{\texttt{trmv}, \texttt{trmv}, \texttt{axpy}}
\State $\tilde{H} \gets
\begin{bmatrix} R_k & S_k \\ S_k\T & Q_k \end{bmatrix} + W\T W$
\Comment{\texttt{syrk}, lower triangle}
\State $\begin{bmatrix} \tilde r \\ \tilde q \end{bmatrix} \gets
\begin{bmatrix} r_k \\ q_k \end{bmatrix}
+ \begin{bmatrix} B_k\T \\ A_k\T \end{bmatrix} w$
\Comment{\texttt{gemv}}
\State factor $\tilde{H} =
\begin{bmatrix} L_R & 0 \\ M & \LP{k} \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} L_R\T & M\T \\ 0 & \LP{k}\T \end{bmatrix}$
\label{alg:sqrt-riccati:potrf}
\Comment{\texttt{potrf}, $[u;x]$ order}
\State $\zeta \gets L_R^{-1}\tilde r$
\Comment{\texttt{trsv}}
\State $\begin{bmatrix} K_k & \kappa_k \end{bmatrix} \gets
-L_R^{-\top} \begin{bmatrix} M\T & \zeta \end{bmatrix}$
\Comment{\texttt{trsm}}
\State $p_k \gets \tilde q - M\zeta$
\Comment{\texttt{gemv}}
\EndFor
\Statex \textbf{Forward pass}
\State $x_0 \gets \hat{x}$
\For{$k = 0, \dots, N-1$}
\State $u_k \gets K_k x_k + \kappa_k$
\State $x_{k+1} \gets A_k x_k + B_k u_k + b_k$
\State \textit{(optional)}\; $\pi_k \gets \LP{k}\!\left(\LP{k}\T x_k\right) + p_k$
\EndFor
\State \textit{(optional)}\; $\pi_N \gets \LP{N}\!\left(\LP{N}\T x_N\right) + p_N$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The recursion is fast because its flops are concentrated in a few dense level-3
kernels on small triangular and symmetric operands~\cite{frison2018blasfeo}: one triangular product
(\texttt{trmm}) to form $W$, one symmetric rank-$n_x$ update (\texttt{syrk}) to
build $W\T W$, one Cholesky factorization (\texttt{potrf}) of the result, and a
triangular solve with multiple right-hand sides (\texttt{trsm}) for the gain.
The asymptotic cost is the same as for the classical form,
$\mathcal{O}\!\left(N (n_x + n_u)^3\right)$, but the leading-order flop count is
lower: propagating $P_{k+1}$ through the dynamics takes a triangular product
(\texttt{trmm}) instead of a symmetric one (\texttt{symm}), which saves more than
the additional factorization of the trailing block costs~\cite{frison2013efficient}.
